Multi-Account & Landing Zone Patterns

Account-level architecture patterns for organizations running multiple AWS accounts: structure, networking, centralized security and logging, and account provisioning. These five patterns complement per-service guidance such as IAM Best Practices and VPC Best Practices.

Patterns at a glance

Each pattern solves one structural problem that appears once workloads outgrow a single account. They are designed to be applied together: the structure (Pattern 1) defines where everything lives, networking (Pattern 2) connects it, logging and security (Pattern 3) watch over it, vending (Pattern 4) creates new accounts safely, and cross-account roles (Pattern 5) let work legitimately cross boundaries.

1. Organizations structure

Accounts as the primary isolation and billing boundary, organized into Security, Infrastructure, Workloads, and Sandbox OUs with SCPs inherited through the tree.

2. Hub-and-spoke networking

Transit Gateway as a central hub for workload, shared-services, on-premises, and inspection VPCs — one attachment per connected VPC.

3. Centralized logging & security

A tamper-evident Log Archive account plus a Security Tooling account with delegated administration, so workloads cannot erase their own evidence.

4. Account vending

Control Tower Account Factory provisions new accounts with preventive, detective, and proactive guardrails applied at creation.

5. Cross-account roles

Temporary, auditable access across account boundaries via sts:AssumeRole with scoped trust policies instead of long-lived keys.

Tip

Decide account structure from your threat model and operating requirements rather than team size alone — small, low-risk workloads may reasonably use fewer accounts, but production/non-production separation and centralized logging deserve early attention.

Pattern 1: AWS Organizations multi-account structure

AWS Organizations makes the account — not an IAM policy boundary — the primary isolation and billing unit, with guardrails inherited through organizational units.

Why this pattern?

A single AWS account with IAM-only separation has a weaker blast-radius boundary between workloads. AWS Organizations makes accounts the primary isolation and billing boundary and lets SCPs, resource control policies, tag policies, and Control Tower controls inherit through roots/OUs.

Note

SCPs set the maximum available permissions but do not grant access, do not constrain principals in the management account, and do not affect service-linked roles. Budgets are configured against accounts, tags, or other cost dimensions rather than attached to an OU as an Organizations policy.

Top-level OUs

Purpose of each top-level organizational unit in a typical AWS Organizations structure.

OUPurpose
SecurityLog Archive (immutable, write-once), Audit/Security Tooling (GuardDuty, Security Hub delegated admin)
InfrastructureNetwork account (Transit Gateway, VPN/Direct Connect), Shared Services (CI/CD, artifact repos)
WorkloadsOne account per application/team per environment — isolates blast radius and billing
SandboxIsolated, auto-expiring accounts for experimentation
Trade-off

Every account adds operational work — budgets, policy attachments, Config/control status, IAM Identity Center assignments, and cross-account resource sharing/networking. Small, low-risk workloads may reasonably use fewer accounts, but production/non-production separation and centralized logging/security should still be decided from the threat model and operating requirements rather than team size alone.

When not to use

Don't fragment into per-microservice accounts for small teams — the management overhead (SCPs, budgets, guardrails per account) outweighs isolation benefits at low account counts.

Pattern 2: Hub-and-spoke networking with Transit Gateway

A central hub keeps connectivity, routing, and inspection consistent across accounts instead of letting peering relationships sprawl.

Why this pattern?

Without a hub, connectivity can grow into many peering relationships and duplicate on-premises, internet-egress, or inspection paths where each VPC needs them. That makes consistent network policy and routing harder to operate across accounts, although simple environments can intentionally use peering and decentralized egress. Transit Gateway provides a hub-and-spoke option: one attachment per connected VPC, centralized routing and inspection paths where required, and route tables that can segment environments. Treat that segmentation as a control to test alongside VPC route tables, appliance behavior, and security policies — not as an automatic guarantee of non-reachability.

Trade-off

Transit Gateway is a highly available Regional managed service, but the centralized routing design, inspection VPC, NAT gateways, and appliances become shared dependencies. Transit Gateway attachment/data processing and centralized inspection/egress charges apply to traffic paths, and an incorrectly designed or undersized inspection tier can affect many workloads. For a handful of VPCs with simple connectivity, peering can be cheaper and easier.

Key decisions

Key decisions when centralizing network egress and inspection through Transit Gateway.

DecisionGuidance
Centralized egressCompare NAT gateway, Transit Gateway, cross-AZ, inspection, and operational costs; centralization can reduce NAT count but does not always reduce total cost
InspectionRoute inter-VPC and egress traffic through an inspection VPC with AWS Network Firewall for centralized policy
Route isolationUse separate Transit Gateway route tables per environment (prod/non-prod) to prevent unintended reachability
When not to use

Don't use Transit Gateway for just 2–3 VPCs with simple connectivity needs — VPC Peering is cheaper and simpler. Don't route all traffic through one inspection VPC without capacity planning — it becomes a throughput bottleneck.

Pattern 3: Centralized logging & security tooling

Separate the custodian of the evidence from the accounts that generate it, so a compromised workload cannot erase its own trail.

Why this pattern?

If CloudTrail, Config, and VPC Flow Logs are stored in the same account that generates them, an attacker (or a careless admin) with sufficient privilege in that account can disable logging, delete the trail, or purge the S3 bucket before the intrusion is detected — the evidence trail and the thing it's supposed to hold accountable share the same failure domain. Similarly, running GuardDuty, Security Hub, and Macie independently per account produces N disconnected dashboards with no org-wide view of findings, making it easy to miss a pattern that only shows up across accounts. Centralizing logs to a dedicated Log Archive account with no human write access, and delegating security tooling administration to a separate Security Tooling account, separates "generate and act on findings" from "custodian of the evidence," so a compromised workload account can't erase its own trail.

Trade-off

This adds a permanent cross-account data pipeline (log delivery, resource shares, delegated admin relationships) that must be verified continuously — a broken CloudTrail organization trail or a misconfigured resource policy can silently stop log delivery from a subset of accounts, and troubleshooting "why don't I see logs from account X" now spans two accounts instead of one. For a single-account setup or a proof-of-concept with no compliance requirement, this is more centralized infrastructure to maintain than the risk currently warrants.

Key decisions

Key decisions for centralizing logs and security tooling across accounts.

DecisionGuidance
Log Archive accountReceives organization trails and selected logs; use S3 versioning/Object Lock retention and tightly scoped roles when immutability is required
Delegated adminGuardDuty, Security Hub, Macie delegated to the Security Tooling account for org-wide visibility
AlertingSecurity Hub findings → EventBridge → SNS/ChatOps for real-time triage
When not to use

Don't skip a dedicated Log Archive account "to save cost" — commingling logs with workload accounts undermines tamper-evidence and audit trust.

Pattern 4: Account vending with Control Tower

Standardized, automated account provisioning with baseline guardrails applied at creation.

Why this pattern?

Accounts created outside the governed workflow can miss the organization's baseline until they are enrolled. Control Tower Account Factory automates account creation/enrollment and baselining in a registered OU, including mandatory and enabled controls. Provisioning and auto-enrollment are workflows with eventual consistency — not an atomic guarantee — so automation must wait for a successful governed/enrolled state before deploying workloads.

Trade-off

Adopting Control Tower means accepting its landing-zone structure and mandatory controls, which can interact with existing SCPs or OU layouts. Retrofitting it onto an established organization requires prerequisite checks, enrollment, and drift remediation. For a small, stable account estate, a different automated baseline may be sufficient, but a manual checklist alone provides weak drift prevention and evidence.

Control types

Control Tower calls these controls. Control behavior (preventive, detective, proactive) is separate from control guidance (mandatory, strongly recommended, elective).

Guardrail typeExample
PreventiveBlocks actions, implemented with SCPs or resource control policies
DetectiveDetects noncompliant resources, generally with AWS Config rules
ProactiveBlocks noncompliant resources before CloudFormation provisions them, using CloudFormation hooks
Mandatory guidanceAlways applied and cannot be disabled; other controls are strongly recommended or elective
When not to use

Don't use an arbitrary account-count threshold; adopt Account Factory, AFT, or another tested vending pipeline when repeatability and governance justify it.

Pattern 5: Cross-account access via IAM roles

Once workloads are split across accounts, legitimate cross-boundary access should be temporary, scoped, and auditable — not standing credentials.

Why this pattern?

Once workloads are split across accounts (Pattern 1), something still needs to legitimately reach across the account boundary — a CI/CD pipeline deploying to prod, a shared-services team troubleshooting a workload account, a SaaS vendor accessing a customer's resources. The naive fix is IAM users with long-lived access keys per target account, but that recreates exactly the risk multi-account isolation was meant to prevent: a leaked key grants standing access with no expiry, no MFA step, and often no per-account scoping, and it has to be manually rotated and revoked in every account it was copied to. sts:AssumeRole with a scoped trust policy makes access temporary and auditable (every assumption is a distinct CloudTrail event with a session name). Removing the trust or source role prevents new assumptions, but already issued role sessions continue until expiry unless they are explicitly revoked; plan for credential and IAM Identity Center session revocation in incident procedures.

Trade-off

Cross-account role chains add a layer of indirection that makes permissions harder to reason about — "who can actually touch this resource" now requires tracing a trust policy in account B back to a role or permission set in account A, and short session durations or MFA conditions on sensitive roles introduce friction (re-authentication, session expiry mid-task) that a standing IAM user never had. For infrequent, low-risk one-off access, the setup cost of a dedicated cross-account role can exceed the risk it's mitigating.

Key decisions

Key decisions for setting up secure cross-account IAM role access.

DecisionGuidance
FederationUse IAM Identity Center (SSO) permission sets over long-lived IAM users with cross-account keys
Trust scopeTrust policies should name specific principals/roles, not entire accounts, where feasible
Session durationSet short MaxSessionDuration for sensitive roles; require MFA condition for break-glass roles
AutomationCI/CD pipelines assume a deployment role per target account rather than holding per-account static credentials (see CI/CD Best Practices)
When not to use

Don't create cross-account IAM users with long-lived access keys — always prefer role assumption.
